# VANSTRUM_FORECASTING_1927: Forecasting Stock Market Trends (Kenneth S. Van Strum, 1927)
Book file: books/public-domain/31_VanStrum_Forecasting-Stock-Market-Trends_1927.pdf  |  Note: books/notes/31_VanStrum_Forecasting-Stock-Market-Trends.md

Page refs are PDF pages. PDF page = printed page + 20 throughout. p.299 (printed 279, "Two averages must confirm each other") is blank in the scan.

**Scope warning:** this book is mostly economic forecasting. Its chart method is one chapter (Ch XIX, p.297-p.310) that restates Dow's theory as Hamilton applied it. The rest of the book (Ch II-XVIII, XX) tests business statistics as barometers; none of that can be read from a price chart, so it is listed in section 7 as not coded.

## 1. The book's idea in one paragraph
Van Strum wants to forecast the major trend (bull or bear market) because almost all stocks follow it (p.27-p.32, p.298). In Ch XIX he summarises Dow's theory in four points: three kinds of fluctuation run at once; the industrial and railroad averages must corroborate each other; when both averages break above a "line" the indication is bullish, and below it bearish; a double top or double bottom predicts a change in trend (p.301). A line is a sideways movement in a narrow range; breaking through its upper limits proves it a line of accumulation (stocks passed from weak to strong hands), breaking through the lower limits a line of distribution (p.300-p.301). His review of every top and bottom from 1897 (Charts 52-53) finds that the averages always give at least one warning before the major trend changes: double or triple tops/bottoms, failure of one average to corroborate the other, and finally the break of a line (p.304, p.307-p.310). He stresses that there are no hard-and-fast rules and that no two turns look alike (p.304, p.310), and he advises the ordinary reader to watch business barometers alongside the averages (p.302-p.303).

## 2. What the book reads on the market (trend, levels, volume, bar/tape) - with p.NN
- **Trend:** the major trend is the bull or bear market. Minor and secondary swings are one of the three fluctuations; secondary moves are "occasionally" predicted but given no great significance, and daily moves are never forecast (p.22-p.24, p.298). A structural definition of trend (higher highs/lows) is not given in the readable text; the missing p.299 may have held more.
- **Levels (line):** a narrow sideways range in the averages with neither up nor down trend. Which side breaks decides whether it was accumulation or distribution (p.300-p.301). Lines form after double tops and double bottoms, sometimes more than one before the change (p.304, p.309).
- **Double top / bottom:** a high, a reaction, a return to near the first high, and a second reaction mean the trend is probably changing; the mirror holds at bear-market lows (p.301). Triple and quadruple tops also occur (p.304). Double bottoms at bear lows can be far apart (p.309).
- **Corroboration:** a new extreme in one average that the other does not match is a warning (p.307-p.308). Both averages must break the line for the signal (p.300-p.301).
- **Volume:** high volume usually goes with high prices and low volume with low prices, but there is "no exact relationship": months of low volume can occur at a bull top and heavy volume near a bear bottom (p.286-p.287). Volume is not part of the Dow-theory rules in Ch XIX.
- **Bar/tape:** nothing. The book uses weekly plots of the averages to show lines and double tops (p.303); it gives no bar, candle or tape signal.

## 3. Setup conditions (numbered S1..Sn; each one observable on a price/volume chart; each with p.NN)
Shown for the end of a bull market (top); the book reads the end of a bear market as the exact mirror: double bottom, line of accumulation, break upward (p.308-p.310).

- **S1** A major advance (bull market) is in place before the setup; the method is applied to major trends only, not to secondary or daily swings (p.298, p.303).
- **S2** Double top: a high, a reaction from it, a second rally that comes back near the first high (or only just through it) without making a clear new high, and a second reaction. Triple or quadruple tops count the same way (p.301, p.304-p.305, p.307).
- **S3** The averages make a line: a sideways movement within a narrow range, showing neither an upward nor a downward trend. It often follows the double top, and there can be two or more lines before the change (p.300, p.304-p.305, p.309).
- **S4 (needs two charts)** Non-confirmation: one average makes a new high (or new low) that the other average refuses to corroborate (p.301, p.307-p.308).
- **S5 (needs two charts)** Both averages (industrials and railroads) make the line and move out of it in the same direction (p.300-p.301).
- **S6** Supporting only: volume tends to be high in high-price phases and low in low-price phases, with exceptions near tops and bottoms; no rule is built on it (p.286-p.287).

Two-average confirmation (choice made): **coded as conditions S4 and S5 with "needs": "two charts"**, because the book makes corroboration one of its four points (p.301) and states the line signal only for "both averages" (p.300). The section that explained it (p.299) is missing from the scan. With one chart, S1-S3 and S6 can be read, but the book's own signal stays unconfirmed.

## 4. Trigger (the book's own signal to act, as the book states it, even if it differs from Abhi's method) - with p.NN
"When the two averages break above a 'line,' the indication is bullish, and below the 'line,' bearish" (p.301). If both break through the upper limits the line has proved to be accumulation and the market may be expected to rise much higher; if both break through the lower limits it is distribution and the market will go lower (p.300-p.301). In the 1899 example, the double top was the "first indication" and the break below the line gave "final notice that the major trend had changed" (p.305-p.307). At bear-market lows, a double bottom followed by a line and an upside break adds to the evidence that a bull market is ahead (p.309). The book also quotes Livermore: in a narrow market, take no interest until prices break through the limits in either direction (p.300).

## 5. Invalidation (what the book says proves the read wrong) - with p.NN
The book has no stated invalidation rule. What it gives:
- The line proves its own character by the side it breaks: a break through the opposite limit means the other reading (accumulation vs distribution) (p.300-p.301).
- A move in one average that the other does not corroborate is not a confirmed signal; in 1901 the rails' new high, "lacking corroboration from the industrials", was followed by a decline (p.307-p.308).
- A double top is a warning, not the change itself: the trend can continue for a long stretch, with more tops and further lines, before the line finally breaks (p.304, p.307).
- Caution: "there are no hard-and-fast rules" and "mechanical perfection" is not to be expected (p.310).

## 6. Book's own exits / stops / size (book text only; in ATP these are the user's dashboard settings, never defaults)
- Stops, targets, R:R, position size, margin: **Book is silent** in the Dow-theory chapter.
- Hold for the major swing, then get out: quoting Livermore, buy and hold in a bull market until you believe it is nearing an end, then "get out of all your stocks" (p.317).
- Trade a diversified list of market leaders rather than single stocks (Livermore's short line of a dozen former leaders) (p.25, p.318-p.319).
- Ignore minor fluctuations; do not take small profits as a bondholder would (p.22).
- When the barometers do not point clearly, favour the bull side (p.317).
- When the stock market is high, short-term notes are a safe place for funds; shifting into long-term bonds is not advised (p.276).
- Commodity forecasting line (not a chart rule): selling about three months after the bearish indication worked better than selling at once (p.98).

## 7. Not coded: time-based rules (C.4) and anything not visible on a chart (news, tips, fundamentals)
- Durations: secondary swings of ten to forty days (p.298); intermediate surges of ten days to three months (p.23); bull markets of two to three and a half years and bear markets of six months to a year and a half (p.24); double bottoms "as much as a year apart" (p.309); line lengths given in weeks or months in the examples (p.304-p.307). Not coded.
- Dated examples of 1899, 1900-1901 and other turns (p.305-p.309). Not coded.
- Business-cycle lengths by country and the claim that US cycles recur more often (p.320-p.322); the book itself says swings cannot be forecast on time alone (p.24, p.322).
- All economic barometers (Ch II-XVIII, XX): business failures, railroad earnings, dividends, commodity prices and the pig-iron/bar-iron/coke forecasting line, price indexes, crop, mineral, iron and steel production, power, building, employment, retail sales, inventories, unfilled orders, car loadings, trade, bank clearings, bank ratios, Federal Reserve rediscounts, commercial-paper and call rates, bond yields, new issues (p.40-p.296, p.311-p.316).
- Numeric rules on those series: 20%/25% production falls, "12 months after the moving average turns up", Ayres' 60% furnaces and 4½% paper rate, Harvard's 1¼% rate move, a 6% rise in the paper-rate average, four months after the NY ratio turns up (p.160-p.164, p.236, p.253-p.254).
- 12-month moving averages and seasonal corrections of statistical series (p.47-p.49, p.313-p.314).
- Hamilton's use of business knowledge when reading the averages (p.302-p.303); "unusual factors" such as corners, earthquakes, failures, wars (p.304-p.305).
- Market-internal data (technical position, floating supply, loaning rates), which the book leaves out (p.312).
- The rule that a barometer within ten points of the top or bottom is "very reliable" (p.304): an evaluation yardstick, not a signal.

## 9. Open points / limits of the book
- The chart method is 14 pages and is a summary of Dow and Hamilton (p.297, p.302). For the fuller reading see `DOW_HAMILTON_1922` and `DOW_NELSON_1902`; this spec is kept separate and adds nothing from them.
- p.299 (printed 279, the section on the two averages confirming each other) is blank in the scan. Whatever extra rule it stated is unknown.
- The book reads two indexes, not a single instrument. How to apply it to one chart: **Book is silent.** S4-S5 need a second chart.
- "Narrow range", how long a line must last, and how far a break must go are not quantified. Charts 52-53 use a 5-point grid, but no threshold is stated (p.306, p.309).
- The book charts the averages weekly to show lines and double tops (p.303). The chart timeframe is an operational setting, not a rule here.
- No stops, targets or sizing in the chart method (section 6).
