# LEFEVRE_REMINISCENCES_1922: The Reminiscences of a Stock Operator, Saturday Evening Post serial (Edwin Lefevre, 1922-1923)
Book file: books/public-domain/12_Lefevre_Reminiscences_Saturday-Evening-Post_1922-1923_article-pages.pdf  |  Note: books/notes/12_Lefevre_Reminiscences.md
Status: independent book strategy; not part of Abhi's method; reference/testing only.

Source text: the first publication in The Saturday Evening Post, 10 parts (10 Jun to 16 Dec 1922) plus 3 "Stock-Market Manipulation" articles (Feb-Mar 1923). **This is the magazine text, not the 1923 Doran book.** Page refs are pages of the combined 81-page PDF, given with the part, e.g. "Part 6, p.37". The trader described is the narrator, Lawrence ("Larry") Livingston.

## 1. The book's idea in one paragraph
**The serial has no complete chart strategy.** It is a narrated career, not a manual. Still, the narrator states one approach several times, with examples and the reasons he follows it. First, know which kind of market it is. Once a move has fairly started the trend is plain, and the trader goes with it (Part 5, p.29; Part 6, p.37). Second, in between-swings periods prices "get nowhere" inside a range. Do nothing there and do not try to anticipate or "push" the next move (Part 6, p.37-38). Third, act when the line of least resistance defines itself: price breaks through the old upper or lower limit and goes on. His examples are a 120-130 range and wheat at $1.10-$1.20 crossing $1.20 (Part 6, p.37-38). Stocks crossing 100, 200 or 300 for the first time are a special case of the same idea (Part 5, p.32; Part 8, p.50). Fourth, test the call with only part of the line. Add only while each lot shows a profit, and judge rightness by how the market takes the orders and how price acts after he begins (Part 4, p.27-28; Part 6, p.38). Fifth, once right, sit tight with the main movement (Part 4, p.23-24). What makes this incomplete as a chart strategy: the decision also rests on "general conditions" (trade, money, crops, news), which no chart shows (Part 5, p.29-33). No range size, follow-through size or exit rule is defined. The order-fill tests are tape readings that a bar chart shows only by their result.

## 2. What the book reads on the market (trend, levels, volume, bar/tape) - with p.NN
- **Trend:** Prices move along the line of least resistance. They go up if there is less resistance to an advance than to a decline, and the reverse (Part 6, p.37). The whole list moves with the main current, so the kind of market comes first (Part 5, p.29). A bull market does not end in one blaze. The first warning is that the leaders react from the top and do not come back while new leaders carry on (Part 8, p.51). In a down move, the end of the usual prompt rallies showed the turn. While prompt rallies to new highs still came, the line was still upward (Part 9, p.56-57).
- **Levels:** The limits of a between-swings range, his "resistance points" (Part 6, p.37). First crossings of 100, 200 and 300 (Part 5, p.32; Part 8, p.50). Supply waiting at and just above a price, which must be absorbed before the line turns up (Manipulation 2, p.74).
- **Volume / activity:** An advance that gets stronger as he buys, "without any spurt" (Part 4, p.26). A rise with a great power of absorption, which is the time to sell a big line (Part 9, p.55). Heavy activity that no longer lifts the price, a "water-logged" stock (Manipulation 2, p.73, p.75).
- **Bar / tape:** How his own orders fill. Fills at rising prices mean he is right. A whole lot filled at one price means something is wrong (Part 4, p.27). Selling taken in small lots with a disproportionate fall means no buying power (Part 9, p.55). A rise that continues through heavy selling means real buying (Part 4, p.28; Part 9, p.58). Whether a stock "acts right" for the kind of market and for its group (Part 4, p.22; Part 9, p.55-56).

## 3. Setup conditions (numbered S1..Sn; each one observable on a price/volume chart; each with p.NN)
These are the narrator's conditions as far as a chart can show them. The text does not number them. The conditions it also needs from "general conditions" (fundamentals, news) are left out and listed in section 7.

- **S1** The general market (and the stock's group) is in a clear trend that has "fairly started": swings rising in a bull market, falling in a bear market. Trade only in that direction (Part 4, p.23-24; Part 5, p.29; Part 6, p.37).
- **S2** The stock moves with its group and the general market, as its group leader does. It is not a laggard that holds back or declines while the group leader and the rest of the group advance (Part 9, p.55-56).
- **S3** Before the move, price has been in a narrow sideways "get-nowhere" range. Clear upper and lower limits are marked by repeated turns: reactions from the top limit and rallies from the bottom limit (Part 6, p.37-38).
- **S4** The stock "acts right" for the trend. In a bull market it rises readily and reactions are shallow and soon recovered. It rises steadily without sudden spurts as activity grows (Part 4, p.22, p.26; Part 9, p.55). In a bear market the mirror image applies: rallies falter, and declines follow without prompt rallies (Part 5, p.29-31; Part 9, p.56-57).
- **S5** Alternative level: price is approaching a round figure of 100, 200 or 300 that it has never crossed before (Part 5, p.32; Part 8, p.50).

## 4. Trigger (the book's own signal to act, as the book states it, even if it differs from Abhi's method) - with p.NN
- **Book's signal:** act at the moment the line of least resistance "defines itself". In a range, that is when buying at the upper limit is for the first time stronger than selling (or selling at the lower limit stronger than buying), so that price breaks through the old limit and goes on (Part 6, p.37). His worked example: wheat held $1.10-$1.20 for months. When it crossed $1.20 he bought, and he went with it as it rose further (Part 6, p.38). He gave the same rule for wheat to friends: wait, and buy the moment it crosses $1.20, not at $1.14, "because I don't know yet that it is going up at all" (Part 6, p.38).
- **Round-figure variant:** buy as soon as a stock crosses 100, 200 or 300 for the first time (Part 5, p.32; Part 8, p.50). In the Bethlehem case he bought at 98-99, a little before par, because the tape showed the crossing was coming (Part 8, p.50).
- **Short side:** sell on a declining market, never at the top. Sell after a reaction if there is no rally (Part 8, p.51; Part 9, p.57). In 1906 he sold "when the rally began to falter and pause", and later said he should have put his short line out on a declining market (Part 5, p.29).
- **Confirmation, part of the signal:** he takes only part of the line first (one-fifth). He makes a second commitment only if the first shows a profit, and judges by "watching the way the price acts after I begin" (Part 4, p.28; Part 6, p.38). A legitimate breakout runs on without stopping (Part 5, p.32).
- Coded for a chart: a close beyond the upper limit of the S3 range (long) or below the lower limit (short), or a first close above 100/200/300 (S5). The chart then shows follow-through in the same direction without a return to the limit.

## 5. Invalidation (what the book says proves the read wrong) - with p.NN
- After the breakout price stalls and falls back to the breakout level instead of running on. Anaconda crossed 300, touched 302¾ and came back to 301. A legitimate advance would have reached 310 without stopping, so the return meant "precedents had failed" and he was wrong (Part 5, p.32).
- The first lot shows a loss. He has begun wrong, so do not add. The tape that said "up" is now saying "not yet" (Part 4, p.28; Part 6, p.38).
- The stock stops acting right: it lags or declines against its group leader, or it fails to rise when buying comes in (a "water-logged" stock) (Part 9, p.55-56; Manipulation 2, p.73, p.75).
- The character of the reactions argues against the position. In a short, sell-offs are promptly rallied to new highs for the move, which shows an upward line. In a long, leaders react from the top and do not come back (Part 8, p.51; Part 9, p.56-57). He says it is the character of the advance or the decline that decides whether his position is right (Part 9, p.57).
- Price rises in spite of steady selling: support has come in, and the line has turned up (Part 9, p.58; Manipulation 1, p.69).

## 6. Book's own exits / stops / size (book text only; in ATP these are the user's dashboard settings, never defaults)
- **Pyramid on profit:** buy 2,000 at 110. If it goes to 111 he is right, so he buys 2,000 more, and a third 2,000 if it is still rising. At about 114 he stops, waits for the reaction, and adds 4,000 on the rally if the fills come at rising prices (Part 4, p.27). In cotton his line was 40,000-50,000 bales: 10,000 first, 10,000 more at +10 points, 20,000 more at +20 points, and out if the first lots showed a loss (Part 6, p.38). Buy one-fifth of the full line first (Part 6, p.38). The big bet goes down only when he is winning; when wrong he loses only a small exploratory bet (Part 4, p.28; Part 6, p.38).
- **Losses:** cut them at once. At roulette he quit each wheel at $200 lost (Part 1, p.5). Never average a losing position. Sell what shows a loss and keep what shows a profit (Part 7, p.44). For him, deciding he is wrong and getting out are one process (Part 9, p.56).
- **Stops:** he names no stop distance of his own. Pat Hearne's habit is reported: buy 100, add 100 on each 1% rise, stop one point below the last purchase, move the stop up, and accept a 1% reaction as the exit (Part 6, p.38). This is another man's system, not Livingston's.
- **Holding:** sit tight with the main movement. In a bull market, buy and hold until you believe it is near its end. Do not take small profits waiting for a reaction to buy back (Part 4, p.22-24). He stood pat through a foreseen rally rather than cover and re-sell (Part 4, p.24; Part 9, p.58).
- **Exits:** sell a big line when the market can absorb it, which is often near the end of a move and into strength (Part 4, p.28; Part 6, p.40; Part 9, p.55). Cover shorts when complete demoralization suddenly develops (Part 8, p.52; Part 9, p.58). Never try to sell at the top; sell after a reaction if there is no rally (Part 8, p.51). Give up trying to catch the last or the first eighth (Part 4, p.24). Never trade at limit prices: when you want to get out, get out (Part 3, p.18; Part 5, p.33).
- **Size and state of mind:** trade on a scale that lets you take small testing losses calmly (Part 8, p.50). "Sell down to the sleeping point" is Dickson G. Watts's story, which he repeats (Part 6, p.36).

## 9. Open points / limits of the book
- No complete chart strategy. The entry depends on "general conditions" as much as on the tape, and the text never says how much weight each gets. A test of this spec tests only the chart part of the narrator's approach.
- No sizes are given for a "narrow" range, a "legitimate" follow-through or a "shallow" reaction. The Anaconda figures (300, 302¾, back to 301, should have reached 310) and the wheat figures ($1.10-$1.20, crossing $1.20) are single examples (Part 5, p.32; Part 6, p.38).
- The narrator gives the round-figure rule (100/200/300) only from experience in dollar-priced stocks and gives no reason for it (Part 5, p.32; Part 8, p.50). The text does not say whether it applies to other price scales.
- "Activity" in the text means the volume of transactions on the tape. It is read here as volume bars.
- The fill tests (Part 4, p.27-28; Part 9, p.55) can be seen on a chart only by their result, so the spec codes the result, not the test.
- OCR: the exact prices on p.27 (the average and the "something is wrong" fill price) and one wheat price on p.38 are garbled (see the note, section 8). The rules above do not depend on those figures.
- The magazine text is not the 1923 Doran book. Wording and page references do not carry over to any book edition.
