# COWEN_COMMONSENSE_1922: Common Sense in Stock Speculation (Maurice Cowen, 1922)

Page refs are PDF pages (PDF page = printed page + 4).

## 1. The book's idea in one paragraph
Cowen's pamphlet is a "buy the break" method for sound stocks. Do nothing until the general market has a good break, which the book puts at about 10 points (p.12-p.13, p.15). Then buy the chosen stock on its own fixed point decline: about 10 points for a standard dividend payer (p.13), 7 points below any new high in Trader No. 2's method (p.20), about 4 points for preferred stocks (p.21), 2-4 points for low-priced stocks (p.15). Buy in lots, the next ones about 2 points lower (p.13, p.20). The book expects a recovery of about half the decline, at which profits are taken, then a secondary reaction of a few points where the stock may be bought again (p.13). After that the trader stays out until the next break (p.16, p.20). Stock selection is mostly fundamental (dividend record, preferred stocks) and is not coded. On the chart, the method reads only point distances from a high, a half recovery and a secondary reaction. There is no trend rule and no confirming bar.

## 2. What the book reads on the market (trend, levels, volume, bar/tape) - with p.NN
- **Trend:** Book is silent on reading trend from structure. It says stocks "usually drop more quickly than they rise" and prices do not go up indefinitely (p.16). The break rule applies "whether in a bull or bear market" (p.13). In the hedge example the trader waits until the market shows "a definite trend one way or another" (p.24), with no definition.
- **Levels:** Measured in points from the latest high: 10 points (standard stocks), 7 points from any new high (Trader No. 2), about 4 points (preferred), 2-4 points (low-priced) (p.13, p.15, p.20, p.21). Preferred stocks range about 10-20 points below par in bear markets and 10-20 above in bull markets (p.18). Investment stocks move in a narrow 3-6 point range while speculative ones move 10 points or more (p.23).
- **Volume / activity:** Among low-priced stocks that broke, choose those that "had shown the most activity" in the last rise (p.15). The word is not defined.
- **Bar / tape:** Book is silent. (The closing advert sells opinions from "study of the tape", p.32, but the book teaches no tape method.)

## 3. Setup conditions (numbered S1..Sn; each one observable on a price/volume chart; each with p.NN)
- **S1 (needs two charts)** The general market (index or average) has broken about 10 points from its recent level; the stock went down "with others in a general break" (p.12-p.13, p.15, p.24).
- **S2** The stock itself has fallen a set number of points from its latest high. Book figures: about 10 points for a standard dividend payer (p.13), 7 points from any new previous high (Trader No. 2, p.20), about 4 points or more for a preferred stock (p.21), 2-4 points for a low-priced stock (p.15).
- **S3** Before the break, the stock had a rise with strong upward tendency and was among the most active stocks in that rise (applied by the book to low-priced stocks) (p.15-p.16).
- **S4** The stock was not bought at the crest of a great bull wave, and has not just broken out of a long narrow range and roughly doubled (the book calls the latter "temporary top prices" for latecomers) (p.15, p.24).
- **S5** Expected path after the break: a recovery of about half the decline, then a secondary reaction of a few points, where the book says the stock may be bought again (p.13, p.24).

## 4. Trigger (the book's own signal to act, as the book states it, even if it differs from Abhi's method) - with p.NN
The trigger is the break itself, with no confirmation: buy the first lot when the stock reaches the set point decline during a general break (S1 + S2), "purchasing the first lot on a 10 point break, the second about 2 points lower" (p.13); Trader No. 2 buys "the first lot on a 7-point break from any new previous high level" (p.20). A second entry is allowed on the secondary reaction after the half recovery (p.13). This differs from Abhi's method, which waits for a commitment candle.

## 5. Invalidation (what the book says proves the read wrong) - with p.NN
- The stock was bought blindly on the crest of a great bull wave; then the book does not expect recovery to the price paid (p.24).
- The stock is highly speculative rather than investment grade: the book says take the loss and switch to an investment stock (p.24). This is a stock-class judgment, not a chart rule.
- The decline continues past the planned lots. Trader No. 2 never needed his fourth lot (p.20), and the book plans for about a 15-point decline in a standard stock (p.13). Book is silent on what to do beyond that, except holding sound stocks outright or switching (p.24).
- Price leaves the old narrow range and doubles: the book treats this as late buying at temporary tops (p.15).

## 6. Book's own exits / stops / size (book text only; in ATP these are the user's dashboard settings, never defaults)
- Exit: take profits when the stock recovers about half the decline (p.13). Fixed point profits in the trader stories: 2 points (Trader No. 1, p.20), 3 and 7 points on scaled lots, 5 points on a single lot, 2 points on the first lot when all lots were bought (Trader No. 2, p.20), 4 points (Trader No. 4, p.21), 5 points on preferred bought on a 5-point reaction (p.18).
- Stops: Trader No. 2 in the bad-speculation chapter found 2-point stop-loss orders were hit before the price rebounded and replaced them with a short sale in a second account (p.23-p.24). The book recommends that method only to active margin speculators, not investors (p.24). Book gives no stop rule of its own.
- Size: split the intended amount into two or three lots: first on the 10-point break, second about 2 points lower, third lower still (p.13); never buy all you can afford at once (p.13, p.16). Diversify (p.15). Example capital split: $1,000 low-priced stocks bought outright, $1,000 standard stocks (p.15-p.16).
- Margin: buy low-priced stocks outright (p.14); preferred on 20-point margin, keep reserve for panics (p.12, p.18); "heavy margins" on speculative purchases (p.12).
- After profits, stay out until the next break (p.16, p.20).

## 7. Not coded: time-based rules (C.4) and anything not visible on a chart (news, tips, fundamentals)
- Time statements: "four weeks later", "two weeks later", "3 to 5 opportunities during a year", wait "many weeks", dividend record "for a period of say 5 years or more", 30-day options (p.11, p.16, p.20, p.21, p.25).
- Fundamentals: dividend record and rate, preferred vs common, earnings warranting dividends, avoiding dividend-cut rumours, named lists of stocks (p.11-p.13, p.15, p.17, p.27-p.28).
- News as the cause of breaks (crop reports, "Mr. Morgan was reported sick", Interstate Commerce Commission) (p.19-p.20).
- Margin and loan yield calculations (p.17-p.19).
- Avoidance advice: short selling, puts and calls, curb, mining and oil stocks, tipsters, brokers (p.21-p.22, p.25-p.30).
- Point figures are absolute 1922 price points; the book gives no percentage version, so any scaling is not from the book.

## 9. Open points / limits of the book
- Thin chart method: no trend reading, no level or demand area, no confirming bar. Selection depends mainly on fundamentals, which are not coded.
- Point figures are absolute and tied to 1922 prices of about $10-$100 per share. Book is silent on scaling them to other price levels.
- "General market fell about 10 points" (S1) is not defined against any named average.
- "Activity" (S3) is not defined; read here as price movement plus volume.
- The "recovery of about one-half" is stated as near-certain by the author (p.13) without data. A test of this spec tests that claim.
