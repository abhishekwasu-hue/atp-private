## 1. The book's idea in one paragraph
**This book has no complete chart strategy.** It is a short essay on how the mental attitude of traders and investors moves prices. It has no charts, no indicators, no entry rules and no defined trigger. What it gives is a description of the "speculative cycle": who holds stock at each stage of a swing. The swing starts in dullness with tiny moves. In a real advance the dips stay small. The public buys late, large holders sell into that demand near the top, the decline runs faster than the advance, and a "bargain" break ends in a quick rally and a sag back into dullness (p.19-p.31). Selden says the same forces work at every swing size (p.31). In Chapter VII he explains reactions through standing scale orders. A reaction in an uptrend lasts until the excess floating supply is absorbed by buy orders below. Then the advance meets less resistance and makes a new high (p.110-p.111). While this "support" is present, declines are cautious and activity falls off at lower prices. Late in the bull move the opposite shows: prices fall easily on larger activity and rallies are sluggish (p.112-p.113). The rest of the book is about the trader's own bias: judge the position *of* the market, not your position *in* it (p.80). The conditions below are limited to these observable descriptions. They are the book's read of a market, not a trading system.

## 2. What the book reads on the market (trend, levels, volume, bar/tape) - with p.NN
- **Trend:** The book describes a real advance by its reactions. They are "of the most trifling character", and when a real one comes it looks "too weak to buy" before price starts up again "with a sudden leap" (p.21). In an upward general trend, each reaction is followed by an advance that meets "far less resistance" and reaches a new high (p.111). After the top, "the decline is usually more rapid than the previous advance", and sharp short-covering upturns do not change the downward course (p.28-p.29). The same pattern holds at every swing size (p.31). For the active trader the guiding phrase is "go with the tide" (p.117) and "catch the trend of sentiment" (p.125).
- **Levels / areas:** "Support" is standing buy orders under the market, and "pressure" is standing sell orders above it (p.110, p.112-p.113). Near the top, buying on dips and profit-taking on rallies keep prices "within comparatively narrow limits" (distribution). A similar period of accumulation often follows a bear market (p.111-p.112). At the "bargain day" the falling price strikes "an area honeycombed with stop loss orders", and large buyers take the stock there (p.30-p.31).
- **Volume / activity:** In a supported bull market "very little stock comes out on declines" and activity falls off at lower prices, while advances draw a following and activity increases (p.112-p.113). Toward the end of the bull market, prices go down "easily and on larger transactions" while advances are sluggish (p.113). In a bear market, activity falls off as prices go up (p.113).
- **Top behaviour:** "everybody seems to be buying". Stocks act erratically: one leaps, one slips back, one "churns violently in one place". A sudden lurch down is called a "healthy reaction", and the general average now rises "very slowly, if at all" (p.26-p.27).
- **Bottom behaviour:** The short interest is on average largest at low prices (p.30). The "bargain day" break is followed by a quick rally, and then prices "sag again" into lethargy (p.30-p.31). In panics a false bottom comes first (p.96), and once the turn appears waiting buyers rush in and the recovery is rapid (p.99). The end of a bear market shows as "prices rebound quickly and sharply from declines" (p.113).
- **Tape:** A "hole" is an active stock that suddenly cannot absorb a modest offering and slips half a point or a point. Professionals read it as an over-bought market (p.88). This is tape-level (see section 7).

## 3. Setup conditions (numbered S1..Sn; each one observable on a price/volume chart; each with p.NN)
These are the book's observable descriptions of a rising market and of an ending decline. The book does not combine them into a setup.

- **S1** Rising trend in which pullbacks stay small and short, and each pullback is followed by a new swing high (p.21, p.111).
- **S2** The pullback in that rising trend is "supported": price goes down cautiously, ranges and activity (volume) fall off at lower prices, and the following advance draws more activity (p.112-p.113).
- **S3** Start of a swing from dullness: a stretch of small fluctuations on low activity, then small rises that are pulled back near the old level, then another rise a little higher than the first (p.19-p.20).
- **S4** A narrow sideways range after a long decline (possible accumulation) or after a long advance (possible distribution) (p.111-p.112).
- **S5** End of a decline: a sharp break to a new low through the prior lows (the "bargain day"), followed by a quick rally (p.30-p.31). The book adds that the rally may sag back into dullness, so S5 alone is not enough.
- **S6** End of a bear market: prices rebound quickly and sharply from each decline (p.113).

## 4. Trigger (the book's own signal to act, as the book states it, even if it differs from Abhi's method) - with p.NN
**Book gives no explicit entry signal.** The nearest statements are these:
- A reaction in an upward trend ends once the surplus floating supply "is absorbed by standing orders". Then "the market is ready to start upward again", meets less resistance than on the reaction, and rises to a new high (p.110-p.111). After a real reaction in a rising market, price "often" starts up "with a sudden leap" (p.21).
- For the active trader: "go with the tide" (p.117) and "endeavor to catch the trend of sentiment" (p.125).
- Commit "only a part of his full commitment at the moment when he feels most confident" (p.124). "When in doubt, keep out of the market" (p.125).
These are recorded as the book's guidance, not as a coded trigger.

## 5. Invalidation (what the book says proves the read wrong) - with p.NN
- Late-bull change: prices go down easily on larger transactions while advances are sluggish and meet opposition at higher levels (p.113).
- Scale buying is withdrawn and replaced by selling on a scale up. From then on it is "easier to produce declines than advances", and a bear market follows (p.111).
- Top behaviour: stocks churn in place, a sudden lurch down appears, and the average rises very slowly if at all while large holders sell (p.26-p.27).
- After the top the decline runs faster than the advance, and sharp upturns from short covering do not reverse it while the floating supply remains (p.28-p.29).
- For S5: the quick rally after the "bargain" break sags back into dullness (p.31). In a panic, a low that "nearly every one" thinks is low enough may be a false bottom, with prices going lower still (p.96).

## 6. Book's own exits / stops / size (book text only; in ATP these are the user's dashboard settings, never defaults)
- Take profit "when the upward movement shows signs of culminating, regardless of your own deal" (p.81).
- Fixed profit/loss point figures (e.g. seven points profit, two and a half points loss) are called arbitrary and foolish (p.81-p.82). Book is silent on any stop distance of its own.
- "If the market is going down the trader must sell, no matter whether he has a profit or a loss" (p.80).
- Size: "do not trade so heavily as to become anxious" (p.125). Commit only part of the full position when most confident, and hold the rest in reserve (p.124).
- "Refuse to follow the market beyond what you consider a reasonable climax" (p.126).
- Keep enough capital in liquid form. Being "tied up" is the great cause of loss in panics (p.96).

## 7. Not coded: time-based rules (C.4) and anything not visible on a chart (news, tips, fundamentals)
- Time statements: distribution absorbed "within three or four days or a week". Prices around top figures "for weeks or months". A range kept "for a month or more". The "very day" scale orders are withdrawn. Staying out a few days (p.27, p.112-p.113, p.120). Not coded.
- News and discounting: a market that ignores good news is "full of stocks", and one that will not fall on bad news is "bare of stocks" (p.40). One considerable move per news item (p.44). Extreme prices come with the most widely spread news (p.75). Discounting and over-discounting (p.61-p.76). These need news, which is not on the chart.
- "They" and who holds stock: floor traders, pools, big interests, short interest size (p.30, p.45-p.59). Not visible on a chart.
- Scale orders, "support" and "pressure" as orders. Only their effects are coded (S2, S6) (p.108-p.113).
- The "hole" (tape-level slip on a modest offering) (p.88). Not visible on a bar chart.
- Fundamentals and money market: liquid capital, call money, bank figures, bonds (p.97, p.106). Not coded.
- Personal bias: judge the position of the market, not your position in it (p.77-p.92). Mental attitude and the five closing suggestions (p.115-p.126). Trader conduct, not chart reading.
- Dated historical examples (p.68-p.70). Not general rules.

## 9. Open points / limits of the book
- No complete chart strategy, no defined entry, and no stop rule. A test of this spec tests Selden's description of market behaviour, not a trading method he gave.
- "Activity" in the book means the volume of transactions (p.112-p.113). It is read here as volume bars.
- The book gives no sizes for "small" pullbacks, "narrow" ranges or "sharp" rebounds. Book is silent.
- Support and pressure are order flow. A chart shows only their effects, and the book says the withdrawal of scale orders is found by close watching of transactions (p.112), which is tape work.
