## 1. The book's idea in one paragraph
**This is the "making a line" breakout as Carret reports it, together with the Dow-average version he gives in Ch VI. Carret is partly sceptical of both (section 9).** A stock that trades in a narrow range for a considerable time is said to be "making a line". Tape readers read a cross of either boundary as the start of a larger move that way: above the top is bullish, below the bottom is bearish (p.78-79). Carret then shows two upside breakouts that failed badly (p.80, p.82). He says no one can tell how wide or how long a line must be, and that the line may be accumulation, distribution, accident or a misguided pool (p.81-82). He gives the idea more weight on the averages. The whole market cannot be manipulated, so a line in the averages and a break out of it "is likely to be significant", but only if the other average confirms (p.116-117). His worked example is the 1921 bottom: a long decline, then a narrow range with lows that stop falling, then a break above the range by one average, confirmed later by the other (p.117). He also quotes Hamilton's 1926 top read: a double top in one average, with the other average failing to confirm a new high (p.119-120). Carret's own approach rests on values, major trend and diversification. He presents the line method as one input that is never enough alone (p.116, p.129, p.152).

## 2. What the book reads on the market (trend, levels, volume, bar/tape) - with p.NN
- **Trend:** Stocks keep an upward or downward trend for long periods with minor interruptions, shown by the averages (p.90). The long-pull trader only has to recognise major movements "when they were fairly under way" (p.113). One- and two-point "ripples" cannot be forecast (p.88-89, p.94).
- **Levels / areas:** The line's upper and lower boundaries (110 and 118 in his example) (p.78-79). On the averages, the high and low of the narrow range after a long decline (p.117), and the prior top in a double top (p.119).
- **Volume:** Tape readers claim that "heavy transactions" in a narrow range over an extended time carry meaning (p.78). Carret gives weekly volume figures for Hudson Motors inside its range but draws no rule from them (p.80).
- **Bar / tape:** Single prints on the tape mean little, because the reader does not know who is behind them (p.77-78). Book is silent on single-bar signals.
- **Breadth across stocks:** At the 1921 bottom month many stocks made their yearly lows and none made highs (p.173). Not a single-chart cue.

## 3. Setup conditions (numbered S1..Sn; each one observable on a price/volume chart; each with p.NN)
- **S1** Price (a stock or an average) has moved within a narrow sideways range for a considerable period, making a "line" with clear upper and lower boundaries. The book gives no width or length (p.78, p.81).
- **S2** (Dow version, bottom case) The line forms after a long, broad decline, and inside the line the later lows stop going lower (the book's "triple bottom" example) (p.117).
- **S3** Heavy volume traded inside the narrow range over that period. This is the tape readers' claim, and Carret does not accept it (p.78, p.81).
- **S4** (Dow version) The other average forms a line at the same time and later breaks the same way. Needs two charts (p.117).
- **S5** (Dow version, top case, Hamilton as quoted) After a long rise, one average makes a double top with a secondary reaction between the two highs, while the other average fails to make a new high, rallies to just below its prior high, then reacts again. Needs two charts (p.119-120).

## 4. Trigger (the book's own signal to act, as the book states it, even if it differs from Abhi's method) - with p.NN
- **Single stock (tape readers' version):** price crosses either boundary of the line. A cross above the top is read as a buy "for a substantial further rise"; a drop below the bottom is read as a probable further decline (p.78-79). Carret reports this as the tape readers' view and argues against it (p.80-82).
- **Averages (Dow version):** the averages break out of the narrow range, and the break counts only when the other average confirms it (p.117). In the 1921 example, the industrial average crossed above its range high first, and the railroad average confirmed late in the same month (p.117).
- **Top (Hamilton as quoted):** the double top in one average, plus a rally in the other to a figure close to but below its prior high followed by a new reaction, "would come near to indicating" the end of the bull movement (p.119).

## 5. Invalidation (what the book says proves the read wrong) - with p.NN
- After an upside break, price falls back through the range and far below it. In the Hudson Motors example the stock broke out of its range to about 122, then broke to 103 the next session and was in the 80s within a month (p.80).
- An upside break while the price is far above what earnings support. Devoe & Raynolds broke out of its range, then fell from about 104 to 40 within the same month (p.82). (The earnings part is not on the chart; see section 7.)
- The line turns out to be distribution in a high market area rather than accumulation (p.81). The chart cannot show which in advance.
- For the Dow version: the other average does not confirm the break (p.117, p.119).
- For a bullish reading at a top: the averages fail to exceed their prior highs (Hamilton: the bull market resumes dependably only if both averages sell above their highs) (p.119).

## 6. Book's own exits / stops / size (book text only; in ATP these are the user's dashboard settings, never defaults)
- "Be quick to take losses, reluctant to take profits" (rule 5) (p.365). A moderate decline is a sounder reason to sell than a moderate advance (p.375).
- Stop-loss orders: a short at 140 with a stop at 146 is shown being stopped out at 146 before a ten-point fall; a call option is presented as the better insurance (p.343-344).
- The "phantom trader" example misses each top and bottom by ten points and still profits (p.110-113). This is an illustration, not a rule.
- Size and margin: at least ten securities in five fields; at least half the fund in income-producing securities; no more than 25% in securities without regular information; borrow sparingly and only when stocks are low (p.364-365, p.379-380). Pyramiding on 20% margin is shown being wiped out by a reaction (p.134-135).
- Book is silent on a stop placed relative to the line's boundaries.

## 7. Not coded: time-based rules (C.4) and anything not visible on a chart (news, tips, fundamentals)
- Line duration "several weeks" and "a considerable period" (p.78, p.117). Durations are not coded; S1 uses "narrow range" only.
- Bull markets 18-24 months, bear markets 7-18 months, aimless periods up to four years (p.95).
- Market runs three or four days one way then a day or two back (p.89).
- Stock-market turns come several months before business turns (p.109).
- Reappraise every holding at least once in six months (p.364, p.368).
- Seasonal money rates and lagged interest-rate correlations (p.140-144).
- Blast-furnace index, dividend-yield "times line", Fisher commodity index, Babson XY line, commercial-paper rate rules (p.120-129, p.151-152).
- Business conditions, money rates and fundamentals as confirmation of the trend (p.125, p.132, Ch X-XVII).
- Earnings that cannot support the price (Devoe & Raynolds) (p.82).
- News and rumour (the Edsel Ford rumour in the Hudson example) (p.80).
- Insider and pool activity behind a line (p.81-86). It is not visible on a chart.
- Stock selection by group and management (p.171-188).

## 9. Open points / limits of the book
**Carret's scepticism (recorded as the book states it):**
- No one can tell the novice how wide a range must be, or how long it must run, to "make a line". Asking dozens of traders gets "no intelligible response" (p.81).
- "The whole theory of reading the tape is based on a fallacy." The line is supposed to show insider accumulation, but it may be distribution in a high area, an accidental sequence of prices, or a pool with no better knowledge than the public (p.81-82).
- Both of his worked single-stock breakouts failed: Hudson Motors broke up and then collapsed (p.80), and Devoe & Raynolds broke up and then lost more than half its price (p.82). He adds that a defender will always say the range was too narrow or confirmation should have been awaited (p.80).
- Even when a line is real accumulation, insiders and pools are often wrong, and the follower "has no assurance that he will be in good company" (p.83-86).
- Short swings of one or two points are "mere gambling" with the odds against the trader (p.88, p.94).
- He accepts the line more on the averages, because the whole market is too broad to manipulate (p.116-117). Even then he wants the two-average confirmation and agreement with business, blast-furnace, dividend and money indices before forming a conclusion on the major trend (p.125, p.132).
- No infallible mechanical method can exist, and all mechanical guides would at some time have failed the trader who used them mechanically (p.129, p.152).
- A correct read of the general trend is not enough. Individual stocks and groups often go their own way (p.171-188).

**Other limits:**
- The book gives no width, length or volume threshold for S1-S3. Book is silent.
- The book gives no stop relative to the range. Book is silent.
- S4 and S5 need two charts (two averages). With one chart the Dow version stays unconfirmed by the book's own standard.
- The S5 top read is Hamilton's, quoted by Carret with approval (p.118-120). It is not Carret's own formulation.
