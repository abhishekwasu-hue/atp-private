## 1. The book's idea in one paragraph
Gann reads the market from a long high-low record of each stock (monthly, weekly, daily), not from the ticker (p.26-27, p.72-75, p.97). The record shows where supply and demand have acted: old tops and bottoms are resistance levels, and long sideways zones where price swings many times over one range are accumulation or distribution (p.84, p.135). The trend is up while bottoms and tops keep rising and down while they keep falling (p.97, p.130). Gann acts in two ways: he buys near an old support level that holds again (and sells near an old top that holds again), with a stop just beyond the level (p.116-119, p.138); or he buys at the market when a stock leaves its accumulation zone actively on large volume, and buys more when it crosses old tops that stopped it for months or years (the second crossing being "safer") (p.85, p.119, p.138). The read is wrong when a long support breaks, or when a new high falls back below the old tops (p.92, p.104-105, p.120-122).

## 2. What the book reads on the market (trend, levels, volume, bar/tape) - with p.NN
- **Trend:** successive bottoms and tops. Each reaction ending at a higher bottom, with tops also rising, means up; the rule holds on daily, weekly, monthly or yearly records (p.97, p.130). Down is lower tops and lower bottoms, with rallies failing below earlier tops (p.83, p.105). A swing chart of major tops and bottoms shows the long trend (p.196-197). Each stock is judged on its own; it can trend against its group or the averages (p.111-113).
- **Levels:** mark every old top and bottom from a long chart before acting (p.84). Support that held several times over years is a buying level (New York Central 88-90, American Loco 74) (p.116-119). A level where price stopped several times is a selling level (Studebaker 59, American Can 68, Atlantic Gulf 117-120) (p.98, p.119, p.122). An old high level usually gives support the first time price reacts back to it (p.119). A long support, once broken, no longer protects price (p.92, p.120-121).
- **Tops and bottoms:** sharp, flat, round, double and triple tops; a double or triple top is a return to the same high after a real reaction (p.80-81). Late movers are distributed below a sharp top and accumulated a few points above a sharp bottom (p.81-82, p.160).
- **Zones:** many swings over the same range with no new high or low = accumulation or distribution; the longer and wider the zone, the bigger the following move (p.28, p.124, p.131, p.135).
- **Volume:** the motive power; big moves start from large volume at the zone (p.27, p.73). Heavy volume with little price gain = supply meeting demand; a last decline leg on shrinking volume = liquidation ending; a small-volume rally after the low = little stock for sale (p.74-75). Heavy volume at an old high with no progress = supply (p.101). Where volume is not reported, judge it by how often price swings across the same small range (p.202).
- **Bar/tape:** a weekly bar that dips only slightly below the prior bar on large volume and closes near its high shows buying outweighing selling (p.99). A test of an old support on heavy volume that holds marks the level as defended (p.101). Dull, narrow trading after a long move means the move has run its course; near a top it shows buying power fading (p.86, p.159). Narrowing swings while tops and bottoms still fall = downtrend still in force (p.181).

## 3. Setup conditions (numbered S1..Sn; each one observable on a price/volume chart; each with p.NN)
Long side shown; the book applies the mirror for the short side (p.85, p.119, p.138).
- **S1 - Rising structure:** the visible swing lows are rising and the swing highs are rising (each reaction ends at a higher bottom) (p.97, p.130).
- **S2 - Marked old level:** the chart shows a horizontal level where earlier tops or bottoms formed; for a support buy, a level where price has turned up more than once (p.84, p.116-119).
- **S3 - Accumulation zone:** before the move, price has swung up and down many times inside one horizontal range without a new high or a new low (p.135, p.192-196); a longer, wider zone implies a bigger move (p.124, p.131).
- **S4 - Price back at support:** the current price has reacted to the old support level, or to an old high level that price had earlier crossed (first reaction back to it), and has not closed through it (p.119, p.138).
- **S5 - Weakening reaction:** the pullback into the level turns narrow and dull, with smaller bars and smaller volume bars than the advance before it (p.74, p.163).
- **S6 - Defended test:** the bar at the level shows large volume and closes in the upper part of its range, with only a small extension below the prior bar (p.99, p.101).
- **S7 - Active departure:** after a dull, narrow zone, bars become wide and volume bars become large as price leaves the zone upward (p.85, p.27, p.73).
- **S8 - Crossing old tops:** price closes above a level of old tops that stopped it several times over a long stretch of the chart; a second crossing after a reaction back below it counts as the stronger case (p.119, p.138).
- **S9 - Relative strength (optional, needs a second chart):** the stock holds above its old supports while other stocks of its group make new lows (p.114-115).

## 4. Trigger (the book's own signal to act, as the book states it, even if it differs from Abhi's method) - with p.NN
Gann gives two entry signals (long side; short side is the mirror):
- **A. Buy at old support.** Buy when price comes down to an old bottom or support level that has held before and holds again, with the stop under the old base (American Loco at 74; American Can around 24-29 after a higher bottom) (p.116-119, p.138). "A stock nearly always gets support the first time it reacts back to old high levels", so the first reaction to a crossed old top is also a buying point (p.119). The mirror: sell and go short at an old top that holds again (American Can 68, American Loco 117) (p.119).
- **B. Buy when the move starts / when price crosses old tops.** When a stock moves up out of its accumulation zone, "if it is very active and the volume of sales large, do not wait; buy at the market" (p.85). If a stock was dull or narrow near a bottom or top, wait for activity, then buy or sell (p.85). When price crosses old resistance levels held for months or years, buy, or buy more (American Can crossing 68 in 1922; American Loco crossing 117) (p.119, p.138). A second crossing into new high ground is safer to buy than the first, because the first crossing meets selling (Republic Steel 96) (p.138). Mirror: when a stock breaks out of its distribution zone, sell out at the market and go short (p.85).

## 5. Invalidation (what the book says proves the read wrong) - with p.NN
- A long-standing support level breaks: support has been withdrawn; look for a new base (p.92, p.117, p.120-122).
- A move into new high ground quickly falls back below the old tops: a false move (p.98, p.104-105).
- Price keeps failing at the same old high level while other stocks make new highs, then breaks its last support (p.104-105, p.110, p.120).
- Tops and bottoms turn lower; rallies fail below earlier tops (p.83, p.105).
- Heavy volume at an old high with no price progress (supply present) (p.101).
- After crossing, price no longer stays above the old resistance levels; the book keeps the trend up only "as long as it stayed above old resistance levels" (p.119).

## 6. Book's own exits / stops / size (book text only; in ATP these are the user's dashboard settings, never defaults)
- Stop on every trade: about 3 points from the entry, never more than 5 (p.50-51).
- Stop 1-3 points beyond an old level; 3 points above the old top on a short; under the old base on a buy at support (p.119, p.138).
- Move the stop to break even once 3-4 points ahead (p.54).
- Capital: dollars per 100 shares, enough for 7-10 trades; spread risk over 4-5 stocks (p.50-51, p.56-57).
- Pyramiding: add every 10 points with smaller lots each time; pyramid only after the stock leaves the accumulation zone (p.65-66).
- Sell out when a distribution zone breaks (p.85); sell investments when support breaks (p.165-170).
- Cotton: stops 20-40 points, pyramid every 60 points; grain: stops 2-5 cents, pyramid every 4-5 or 7-10 cents after zones are cleared (p.174-175, p.190-191).
- Rejected by Gann: averaging/scale buying, hedging, straddles (p.66-67, p.176).

## 7. Not coded: time-based rules (C.4) and anything not visible on a chart (news, tips, fundamentals)
- Time factor (Gann's undisclosed "most important factor") and annual forecasts (p.137, p.160).
- Moves worked out in 3-4 waves "according to time and vibration" (p.83).
- Clock-time habits: first 30 minutes, 2:30 P.M. evening-up, last-hour margin selling (p.29-32).
- Day-of-week habits: Monday openings, Wednesday-to-Saturday declines (p.31-32).
- Election years and waiting 2-3 days after an election (p.33-36).
- Seasonal pool timing (April-May rise, June-July break) and seasonal crop rules (p.82, p.185, p.189).
- Day counts: buy on the second day's reaction with a 3-point stop because active stocks seldom react more than two days (p.85).
- Time-held rule: after holding below a top or above a bottom for two weeks or more, buy or sell when active in new territory (p.85).
- Loss limited to about two days' fluctuations in active high-priced stocks (p.59).
- Seven Zones of Activity (sentiment stages, not price structure) (p.76-79).
- News, dividends, book value, insiders' publicity, investment advice, forecast-service advert (p.37-40, p.92, p.140-142, p.165-170).
- Volume judged against shares outstanding and floating supply (not on a chart image) (p.27).
- Point-and-figure style 3/5/10-point move charts (a different chart type) (p.130, p.184).

## 9. Open points / limits of the book
- Gann's point distances (3, 5, 10 points) are absolute prices for 1920s US stocks; the book gives no rule for scaling them to other prices or instruments.
- "Many swings", "long period", "very active", "large volume" have no numeric threshold in the book; a chart reader must judge them relative to the visible chart.
- The book gives two trigger types (support buy and breakout buy) and does not rank them except that the second crossing of an old top is safer than the first (p.138).
- The case studies end in January 1923; OCR numbers are often damaged, so single figures quoted here are approximate (see note section 8).
- Chart pages were not read as images; conditions come from the text.
