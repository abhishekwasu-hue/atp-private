## 1. The book's idea in one paragraph
Browne reads the market as broad "tidal swings" and judges where a swing stands from price and volume of transactions. A bear swing ends with sharp declines on great activity, then a "period of rest": dull, narrow, stationary prices a little above the first low, which he calls accumulation (p.68, p.83-p.84). When a stock breaks to a new low on heavy volume, rallies and comes back toward that low, the second decline on "considerably lighter" volume shows the stock went into strong hands (p.80-p.82). As the bull swing starts, volume increases and keeps increasing while prices rise (p.83). Reactions inside the bull swing either sag slowly on falling volume, with activity renewed when the rise resumes, or show a day or two of heavy liquidation and a short rest (p.87-p.88). The book names the reaction near the start of a bull swing as the investor's "best and safest buying opportunity" (p.102). Around turning points the stock that falls less and rises more than others is preferred (p.98-p.99). Money rates, bonds and business data are the book's other tools; they are not on a price/volume chart and are not coded.

## 2. What the book reads on the market (trend, levels, volume, bar/tape) - with p.NN
- **Trend:** the direction of the tidal swing; the word is relative to the size of swing followed (p.101). Bull swings are longer and are interrupted by reactions followed by new highs; bear swings are shorter and sharper, with rallies of about five points in the averages (p.70-p.75, p.102-p.104). The market "has to be pushed up but falls down of itself" (p.75).
- **Levels / areas:** the low of the first sharp decline after a depression and the dull accumulation zone just above it (p.68); the prior low as a test area after a rally (p.80-p.82); the active, little-changed distribution zone at a boom top (p.67-p.68). Investors who bought low and will not sell lower give "permanent support" (p.28).
- **Volume:** rises with wide price moves and with public participation (p.80, p.82). Heavy on the first break into new low ground; lighter on a retest if investors took the stock (p.80-p.82). Low in the period of rest; increasing through the bull swing; heavy in the first liquidating break of a bear swing; falling through the rest of it, with a few days of big volume at its end (p.83-p.84). Increased at the top of a considerable bear rally (p.85).
- **Reactions and rallies:** bull-swing reactions: (a) small bear market with a day or two of active liquidation at the low, then a brief rest, or (b) gradual fall in volume as prices sag slowly, with renewed activity when the rise resumes (p.87). Bear-swing rallies: volume falls on the advance and rises when the fall resumes, or a violent short-covering jump, or an active rise whose volume peters out near the top (p.88).
- **Bar / tape:** Book is silent on single bars. It reads weekly and monthly averages and volumes and says daily graphs show more exceptions (p.86, p.108-p.110). Price moves on the urgency of buyers or sellers, not on the size of orders (p.25-p.27).

## 3. Setup conditions (numbered S1..Sn; each one observable on a price/volume chart; each with p.NN)
- **S1** End of a decline: a sharp fall into new low ground on heavy volume (liquidation; the book adds a few days of big transactions at the end of a bear swing) (p.68, p.80-p.81, p.84).
- **S2** After that low: a period of rest with dull trading, narrow price changes and low volume, prices holding a little above the low of the first decline (accumulation) (p.68, p.83-p.84).
- **S3** Retest: after a rally from the low, price comes back toward the low without making new low ground, on considerably lighter volume than the first decline (p.80-p.82).
- **S4** Bull swing under way: as the advance starts, volume increases and keeps increasing while prices rise; sharp rises come with large volume (p.80, p.83).
- **S5** Reaction inside the rising swing in one of the book's two forms: (a) prices sag slowly on gradually falling volume, or (b) a day or two of active heavy-volume liquidation at the low followed by a brief quiet pause (p.87-p.88).
- **S6** Resumption: activity (volume) renews when the upward swing resumes after the reaction (p.87).
- **S7** The reaction is the first sizeable one after the bull swing began (the book measured it as five points or more in the averages) (p.102).
- **S8 (needs two charts)** Around the turn, the stock shows better resistance than others: its pullbacks are smaller and its advances relatively better (book example: higher high and higher low while the other stock makes lower highs and lows) (p.98-p.99).

## 4. Trigger (the book's own signal to act, as the book states it, even if it differs from Abhi's method) - with p.NN
The book's buying signal is the reaction itself, not a bar: "a reaction of five points or more occurred near the beginning of every bull market. It is on this customary reaction that the investor generally has his best and safest buying opportunity" (p.102). For a buyer who judges the swing has turned, the book says buy at that point rather than average (p.111-p.112); for a buyer on a reaction in a move already under way, buy on a scale or a part at a time (p.112-p.113). The book describes the volume signs of a healthy reaction (S5) and of resumption (S6) (p.87) but does not tie the purchase to a specific volume bar. In this spec the trigger reads: S4 + S5 + S7, with S6 as the book's sign that the rise has resumed.

## 5. Invalidation (what the book says proves the read wrong) - with p.NN
- Price goes below the low that marked the end of the bear swing: the book's stop-order example treats this as a movement that "would prove him wrong" (p.113), and leaves using the stop to the investor (p.114).
- On the return toward the low, activity and wide price moves come back as the market starts down again: the stock sold on the first break was bought by speculators "for a turn" and the burden is not reduced (p.81-p.82).
- The advance shows the bear-rally pattern: volume falling on the rise and increasing when the decline resumes, or volume peaking at the top of the rally (p.85, p.88).
- Boom/distribution: very active market, feverish moves and big transactions with little change in the general price level (p.67-p.68).
- A purchase made on a reaction in the middle of a bull swing that shows a profit and then falls back to the buying price (the book's case for a stop to keep a profit from becoming a loss) (p.114-p.116).

## 6. Book's own exits / stops / size (book text only; in ATP these are the user's dashboard settings, never defaults)
- Stops: whether to use a stop below the prior low "depends on the man" (capital and temperament); whichever method is chosen must be followed consistently (p.113-p.114).
- A stop to keep a profit from running into a loss (book example: bought at 80, up to 85, stop at 80 1/8); more important the higher prices are and for purchases made on mid-bull reactions; not usually desirable at low prices early in a swing (p.114-p.117).
- Averaging: no advantage when buying at what one judges to be the turn; buy on a scale (e.g. 30, 28, 26) or half at a time when buying on a reaction in a move already under way (p.111-p.113).
- Exits: do not hurry to take profits early in a bull swing; the usual loser "takes a small profit and a big loss" (p.77). The book's time-based exit (sell after nearly two years of bull market) is in section 7.
- Short side: short sales, if made, early in a bear swing, not after six months or more of it (p.79).

## 7. Not coded: time-based rules (C.4) and anything not visible on a chart (news, tips, fundamentals)
- Swing durations and dates: tidal swings of 1-5 years, bull about 21 months, bear about 9 months, lists of turning months and periods of rest (p.15-p.18, p.71-p.73, p.83).
- Time-framed reaction rules: in the first year of a bull market or in a bear market, reactions/rallies not more than about five points; in the second year, a reaction of ten points or more then a new high; no extended reaction before a year (p.103-p.104).
- Time-based exits: sell after nearly two years of bull market; buying after about a year of falling prices is comparatively safe (p.78-p.79).
- Cycles: Minor Cycle 3-4 years, Major / 20-year cycle, 10-year capital accumulation (p.46-p.47, p.70).
- Hourly volume habits (first and last hour active, lunch hour quiet) (p.86).
- Money rates: commercial-paper rate highest near stock lows, 6% rate near bull tops (p.34-p.42).
- Bonds: first good bond rally after a panic preceding a stock upswing; bonds declining before stock tops (p.47-p.48). Would need a bond-average chart; not coded.
- Business data: U.S. Steel unfilled orders, bank clearings, railroad earnings, commodity prices (p.50-p.60).
- Stock selection by dividends, earnings growth, industry and listing (p.90-p.97).
- Psychology, news (Lusitania, war fears), pools, floor traders (p.29-p.31, p.62-p.68, p.72, p.88).
- Point sizes (5 and 10 points) are absolute points in the 1902-1917 averages; the book gives no percentage form.

## 9. Open points / limits of the book
- The book's volume figures are monthly and weekly totals for the whole market (p.83, p.86, p.109). Applying S1-S6 to one stock's daily bars goes beyond what the book tested; it says daily graphs show more exceptions (p.86).
- "Heavy", "lighter", "dull" and "sizeable" are not quantified, except the five-point reaction in the 1902-1917 averages (p.102). Book is silent on percentage sizes.
- The trigger is a reaction, not a bar; the book gives no entry bar and no exact entry price.
- The fold-out graph the book relies on is missing from the scan, so the book's own chart examples cannot be checked against it.
- Text comes from OCR of a scan with no text layer; tables were checked from the page images where OCR dropped rows.
