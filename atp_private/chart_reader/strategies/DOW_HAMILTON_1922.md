## 1. The book's idea in one paragraph
Hamilton, Dow's successor at The Wall Street Journal, reads Dow's theory as a "barometer". The market has three movements at once: the primary swing, the secondary reaction or rally that interrupts it, and the daily fluctuation, which is "largely negligible" (p.22-p.24, p.78). The primary trend is read from the swing structure: higher highs for a bull period, and a low below the earlier lows for a bear period (p.50). A new bull market shows as "zig-zag steps" (p.199-p.200). A "line" is a stretch of narrow-range trading, and it can only mean accumulation or distribution (p.24, p.78, p.194-p.195). The line usually forms after the secondary break, not before it, and a line of accumulation then points to the primary move resuming (p.164-p.165, p.173). The railroad and industrial averages must confirm each other (p.24, p.160-p.161, p.180). The book's signal to act is that both averages rise out of the line ("strongly bullish", p.24, p.180). For the speculator it adds a second rule: once the major bull swing is established, buy in "that inevitable period of dulness which develops after a secondary reaction in a major bull swing", and add as the market rises (p.285-p.286). The book's main purpose is forecasting general business. Speculation is a side topic (Ch XIII and XXI).

## 2. What the book reads on the market (trend, levels, volume, bar/tape) - with p.NN
- **Trend:** The book quotes and uses Dow's rule throughout: bull while each high exceeds the earlier highs, bear when a low breaks below the earlier lows (p.50). A primary bull is confirmed when a reaction "does not decline to the old low figures" and price then "recovers to points better than the new high established on the earlier rally" (p.199-p.200). The primary trend still governs during the secondary move, like a tide whose waves recede and then reach further (p.176-p.177).
- **Secondary swing:** The secondary move turns against the trend abruptly, and the primary move resumes more slowly (p.164-p.165). In a primary bull, downswings are "short and sharp, with a halting recovery consuming a longer time than the decline" (p.63). A secondary move can look exactly like a new primary trend (p.165).
- **Shortened pendulum:** After a bear low there is a rally, then a smaller reaction "checked before it reached the old low", then a smaller rally. The swings narrow as the market nears equilibrium (p.152).
- **Line (area):** A line is a narrow range of closing prices "over a sufficient number of days", in either average (p.24, p.180). It gains significance "as the number of trading days increases" (p.78). It is "either one of accumulation or one of distribution", with buying and selling power in equilibrium for a time (p.194-p.195). The move that follows shows whether the market was "bare of stocks or saturated" (p.78).
- **Volume:** In primary moves, volume expands on advances and contracts on declines, but "a sharp reaction in a bull market will often stimulate the volume" (p.158-p.159). Volume is "much less significant than is generally supposed. It is purely relative" (p.199). Hamilton also calls attempts to combine volume with the averages "unnecessary" (p.25).
- **Bar/tape:** No inference can be drawn from a single day's trading (p.194). Floor tape reading is not described (p.166).
- **Double top/bottom:** "by no means infallible, but is often useful" (p.181). Earlier Hamilton says he has not found it "essential or greatly useful" (p.50).
- **Panic-type break:** A sharp fall, a rally of a large part of it (the quoted editorial says 40-60%), then "an irregular sag" back to about the old low that clears out weak holders (p.126-p.127).
- **Dullness:** "Never sell a dull market" is right in a bull market and wrong in a bear market, where the market goes dull after a sharp rally (p.168).

## 3. Setup conditions (numbered S1..Sn; each one observable on a price/volume chart; each with p.NN)
Long side shown. In a bear market the book reverses the reading (line of distribution, break below the line) (p.24-p.25, p.165, p.173).

- **S1** Primary trend up: swing highs exceed the earlier swing highs, and no swing low has broken below the earlier swing lows (p.50).
- **S2** "Zig-zag steps": a rally, a reaction that stops above the previous low, then a new high above the earlier rally high (p.199-p.200, p.152).
- **S3** A secondary reaction has occurred against the primary up trend. It is typically abrupt, short and sharp (p.63, p.164-p.165).
- **S4** After that reaction, a line forms: a narrow sideways range of closes held over many bars. The book places this line after the secondary break, not before it, and its significance grows with its length (p.78, p.164-p.165, p.173, p.180).
- **S5** Dullness after the reaction: quiet bars with a narrow range and low activity, inside the primary bull swing (p.168, p.276, p.285-p.286).
- **S6** Supporting only: volume expands on up-legs and contracts on down-legs in the primary move. A sharp secondary reaction may show higher volume, and the book calls volume "purely relative" (p.158-p.159, p.199).
- **S7 (needs two charts)** The two averages confirm each other. Both show the same primary direction, and both make the line and leave it in the same direction. They need not do so on the same day or in the same week (p.24, p.80, p.160-p.161, p.180).

Two-average confirmation (choice made): **coded as condition S7 with "needs": "two charts"**, because the book makes it central ("the averages must confirm each other", p.80, p.160, p.180). A single chart image cannot check S7. With one chart, S1-S6 can be read, but the book's own signal stays unconfirmed (p.160-p.161 shows one average misleading on its own).

## 4. Trigger (the book's own signal to act, as the book states it, even if it differs from Abhi's method) - with p.NN
The book gives two signals.
1. **Line exit (barometer signal):** "When the two averages rise above the high point of the line, the indication is strongly bullish" (p.24). A movement of the average out of the line "will confidently indicate a change in the general market direction of at least a secondary and even a primary character", which "we can depend upon where either average is confirmed by the other" (p.180). After a secondary decline, "a bull indication after a line of accumulation" points to a new high above the start of that decline, and such a new top is evidence that the bull movement has resumed (p.173). Bear mirror: both averages break below the line (p.24-p.25).
2. **Speculator's entry (Ch XXI):** Once the major bull swing is established, buy in "that inevitable period of dulness which develops after a secondary reaction in a major bull swing". Then add to holdings "as the market rises" rather than buying on the way down (p.276, p.285-p.286).

## 5. Invalidation (what the book says proves the read wrong) - with p.NN
- Both averages break below the line. This indicates "saturation point": a secondary bear move in a bull market, or the start of a primary downward movement (p.24-p.25, p.173, p.180).
- A swing low breaks below the earlier swing lows: a bear period (p.50).
- After the purchase, the stock reacts "carried down by a similar reversal of movement in the general market". The speculator "should take his loss without hesitation" and wait for the next dullness (p.286).
- A signal in one average that the other does not confirm. Hamilton calls this "one of the shortest ways of going wrong" (p.160-p.161, p.181).
- Double top in the averages near the end of an advance gives "strong reason for suspecting that the rise ... is over". The book says it is not infallible (p.181).
- Caution, not proof: a secondary reaction can look "deceptively like the real thing" (p.165).

## 6. Book's own exits / stops / size (book text only; in ATP these are the user's dashboard settings, never defaults)
- Take the loss without hesitation if the stock reacts with the general market after purchase (p.286). A successful speculator's habit: a two-point loss taken "without hesitation if the market did not move his way" (p.88).
- Add to holdings as the market rises. Do not "pyramid" too much, and protect with "stop-loss" orders (p.286).
- Against scale-down buying: buying more on each point of decline contradicts the original judgment (p.279-p.280).
- Margin: ten points margin is "not enough, or nearly enough". Quoting Dow, a man buying 100 shares on 10% margin and stopping his loss at 2% "has lost (with commissions) nearly one-quarter of his capital". A trader with $1,000 should not start with a hundred shares (p.279).
- Short selling "may well be left to the professional" (p.285).
- Exits on the line: Book is silent on a target. Its only measure is "a new figure higher than that from which the secondary decline took its origin" (p.173).

## 9. Open points / limits of the book
- The book reads two indexes, not a single stock or instrument. Hamilton warns that "the average is deceptive for speculation in individual stocks" (p.24). How to apply it to one instrument: **Book is silent.**
- S7 (two-average confirmation) needs a second chart. A single chart image can show only S1-S6.
- "Narrow range" and "sufficient number of days" are not quantified. The 1914 example spans about two points in each average (p.195).
- The line exit is a breakout signal. The book gives no bar-level confirmation, and a single day carries no inference (p.194).
- OCR: the underlined key passages on p.164-p.165, p.173 and p.180 are garbled and were read by sense. The opening of the line definition on p.194 is lost. Quotes were taken only from readable lines.
- The panic pattern (p.126-p.127) and the shortened pendulum (p.152) come from Journal editorials that Hamilton quotes, about the averages at market turns. They are listed in section 2 as reads, not as setup conditions.
